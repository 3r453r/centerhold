I want to begin with the sentence everything I write here hangs on: **there is a way things
are.** Not a way for you and a way for me — a way, full stop, indifferent to preference,
party, or mood. The world was some way before anyone was here to have opinions about it, and
it will be some way after.

That is my philosophical starting point. The practical aim of this essay is broader:
to find a shared framework in which claims can be checked, mistakes corrected, and
coercive rules challenged. You may disagree with my account of why those practices
work. If you accept that shared framework as something worth building while rejecting
the rest of this project, this essay has still achieved something I want.

## 1. What I am claiming, and what I am not

The claim is narrow. *There is a way things are* says nothing about who knows it, how well
anyone can know it, or how confident anyone is entitled to be. Those are separate questions,
and I give them the less comfortable answers: plenty of truths are out of reach, our
instruments are partial, our minds are motivated, and honest inquiry moves toward the facts
slowly and asymptotically when it moves at all. None of that touches the claim. A hidden fact
is still a fact. The number of fish in the Atlantic at this moment is unknown and unknowable
in practice; it is also exactly one number.

Notice what the claim forbids — because a claim that makes no difference anywhere says
nothing; that rule is on my assumptions page ([A0.2](/assumptions#a0-2)), and I apply it to my own claim first. This claim makes no
observation more or less likely than it was; its difference is in what may be inferred. It
forbids *your truth and my truth*
as descriptions of the world. It permits, cheerfully, your perspective and mine, your values
and mine, your experience and mine: perspectives differ because we stand in different places,
not because there are different worlds to stand in. Two witnesses to a car crash disagree,
and we can say something precise about that: at most one of each contradictory pair of their
claims is right. The crash happened one way.

A second commitment on my assumptions page, the one that keeps the map apart from the chosen
path ([A0.D2](/assumptions#a0-d2)), forbids something subtler: the comfortable retreat that
says "well, that is true for science, but this domain is different." A domain where nothing
is the case is not a deeper domain; it is empty of facts, and should say so. Most real
domains are neither wholly one nor the other: part found, part chosen. My own ethics is
mixed in exactly this way. That suffering is bad for the one who suffers, I take to be
found; much of the rest of morality, I take to be chosen. Wherever there is a fact of the
matter, disagreement is a problem with a possible resolution. Wherever there is not, we are
no longer describing — we are choosing, and I insist on saying so out loud. The map is one
register, the chosen path another, and I keep them apart everywhere.

## 2. The referee

Now watch what the sentence *does*. Two people disagree about something that matters — a
dosage, a bridge load, whether the well water is safe. If there is a way things are, their
disagreement has a referee. Not a person: a direction. There is something the dispute is
*about*, sitting outside both of them, that their claims can be checked against. They can be
wrong. They can find out. The argument can *end* — not by one side tiring, but by the world
answering.

Not always, and I will not pretend otherwise. Some disagreements run all the way down to
where argument stops discriminating — each side's last premise against the other's, and
nothing left that the other is obliged to accept. "Last" is a fact about the two of them,
not about the reasons: it marks where these parties stopped, not a place where the chain of
reasons ends. There the world does not answer, or does not answer yet. But notice what my
opening sentence, a mild one as metaphysics goes, still buys you even there, because it is
not nothing. Where those last premises are claims about the world, the world is some way on
the point the two of them stopped short of: of two premises that contradict each other, at
most one is true, and the deadlock is ignorance, not symmetry. Where a last premise is an
aim rather than a claim about the world, the second register applies
([A0.D2](/assumptions#a0-d2)), and there may be no fact to find. That much follows from
the mild sentence alone — which also tells you whom it binds. It binds anyone who grants the
mild sentence too. Against someone who refuses it, the mild sentence is itself my last
premise, set against theirs. That limits what this argument proves; it does not
establish that we cannot cooperate on a practical question. I also expect sustained
inquiry tested against reality to improve our grasp of particular questions — a
further, weaker commitment ([A0.7](/assumptions#a0-7)). A replacement theory must
account for the well-supported successes of its predecessors within their tested
domains, while remaining open to correcting them. That supports an expectation of convergence; it does not guarantee
that this particular ignorance is temporary or that every deadlock will be resolved.
Someone who declines my full metaphysical account may still accept tests that can
correct either of us. We should establish what they actually accept before treating
philosophical disagreement as refusal to answer to evidence.

Every practice we trust works this way. Engineering is argument refereed by collapse.
Medicine is argument refereed by mortality. Navigation is argument refereed by the coastline.
The referee is often slow, often expensive to consult, sometimes cruel — but it is there, and
both parties accept the same ways of consulting it. Those shared tests and standards of
evidence, not a shared metaphysics, are what make their disagreement a *conversation* rather
than a contest. The loser of a refereed argument has not been defeated
by the winner. He has been corrected by the world, and the winner stands corrected-in-advance
on the next question. That is why refereed losing is survivable, even useful. Nobody submits
to a person; both submit to the way things are.

Put as a principle: sharing workable standards of evidence and public rules need not require agreement on every metaphysical foundation. A disagreement about those foundations does not by itself show that someone rejects factual correction or that cooperation with them reduces to force. Where no workable frame can be negotiated in the time available, force may still resolve the conflict without justifying its outcome.

Two investigators could disagree about whether permanently inaccessible reality has
a determinate state and still agree on how to test the water. That practical agreement
does not settle their metaphysical disagreement. It gives them something useful to
do together without settling it first.

## 3. The tell

There is a practical failure we can identify without diagnosing anyone's metaphysics:
replacing an assessment of the claim with allegiance to, or hostility toward, the claimant.

A measurement is on the table. One side dislikes what it shows. Let me list the honest
responses, because there are more of them than the usual fight admits. *The measurement is
wrong, and here is a better one.* *The measurement is right, and here is why it means
something else.* And a third, which I want to protect explicitly: *the measurement is
unreliable, here is why, and I do not yet know the true value.* A broken thermometer can be
thrown out before you own a working one. Who funded a study, how its sample was chosen, who
declined to answer, whether the thing counted is the thing claimed — all of that is evidence
about reliability, and asking after it is not leaving the map. It is doing the map's work.
All three responses accept the referee; all three can lose.

The tell is none of these. The tell is when the story of where a number came from is offered
as a *complete substitute* for looking at the number — when "the number is theirs" is the
whole reply, and no correction the other side could make, no re-measurement by anyone, would
make the result admissible. Such a reply, if that is all there is, contains no claim about
the world. It does not say the world is other than measured, and it does not say the
instrument is broken in some way a better instrument could fix. There, allegiance has
replaced an assessment of the evidence. That is the failure here; it does not establish the
speaker's entire philosophical position. And it is a claim about what would change the
speaker's mind, not about what they happen to say. Someone who cannot say what is wrong with
a number may simply lack the expertise to say it, or may be applying a fair discount to a
source whose track record has earned one. Silence alone is not the tell.

Investigating bias is legitimate. Making a result inadmissible solely because it
comes from the other side, regardless of checks or correction, blocks that shared
inquiry. It need not prove that force is inevitable to be a practice worth opposing.

## 4. What force must answer to

Coercion needs a justification. A factual accusation is one part of that task;
justifying the rule under which someone would be coerced is another. Here is the
policy I defend, also recorded as [A0.P4](/assumptions#a0-p4):

Within a shared framework, coercive rules must be publicly stated and their factual conditions open to scrutiny. This is a necessary safeguard, not a sufficient justification: the rule itself also requires a normative defence. Factual uncertainty calls for justified standards of evidence and procedures for challenge; it does not by itself make every use of force permissible.

A public rule saying that people with blue eyes must be imprisoned could have a
perfectly checkable trigger. Accurate application would not supply a justification
for the rule. We have to defend what the rule permits as well as establish whether
its conditions are met. That defence belongs to policy; it cannot be read straight
off a measurement.

For parties who share the framework, factual scrutiny constrains the application
of a rule: an accusation must answer to evidence about something neither party
controls. The accuser can be wrong, and the process must leave room to challenge
that accusation. This requires neither an infallible instrument nor indisputable
knowledge. An uncertain danger may justify a precautionary response; what evidence
would justify punishment is a further question. The standards and procedures need
their own defence. The existence of an objective fact does not give us automatic
access to it or supply those standards.

That is the bounded connection between truth and force. A claim about what happened
must not become true merely because the more powerful party asserts it. But neither
a correct accusation nor a public rule is enough, on its own, to establish that a
particular coercive response is justified.

So what does defend a rule? My answer is conditional. The norms of a shared life bind
whoever wants into that life, and are silent toward those who don't. Inside it, a
coercive rule is defended when the people who want in could accept it as a term of
living together, whatever else they believe about the world. This is not a new idea.
John Rawls argued, in *Political Liberalism* (1993), that people holding incompatible
worldviews can still share the terms of a political life, and that political power is
fully legitimate only when it is exercised under a constitution whose essentials all
citizens could reasonably be expected to endorse. He hoped for an overlapping consensus:
each worldview endorsing those shared terms from within, for its own moral reasons. The
cooperation I hope for at the end of this essay is close to his in aim: shared terms
across worldviews that do not otherwise agree.

"Could accept" includes grudging acceptance. A rule may cut against someone's
convictions, but not so far that they would rather leave the shared life, because
staying is worth more to them by their own weighing. That is the threshold. The test I
prefer when writing a rule is stricter: if this rule were in place, would I agree to be
its subject? It is the old role-reversal form of the Golden Rule. Both are practical
standards for real people living together, not a claim to have found the morally right
rule for perfectly informed, idealised agents.

The threshold only works for people who can choose. If you can neither reject a rule
nor leave, the rule is not a policy you are party to; for you it is part of the terrain,
like the weather. It is still a policy for whoever imposes it, and they owe an answer.
Imposed on people who cannot realistically leave, a rule has to pass the stricter test:
would I, the one imposing it, agree to be its subject? And it stays bounded by the limits
that never depend on anyone's agreement, the suffering floor and the ban on torture.
Taking someone's options away when you could leave them open is itself a wrong, even if
the person does not know what they are missing and is content: seen from the full view,
they are deprived of ways they could have grown.

Run two rules through both tests. A speed limit: the driver who likes speed accepts it
grudgingly rather than emigrate, so it passes the threshold; and if I picture a child
near the road, I would agree to be fined under it, so it passes the stricter
test too. The blue-eyes rule: a blue-eyed person who could leave would rather go than
live under it, so it fails the threshold; for one who cannot leave the threshold is
silent, and the rule meets the stricter test instead, which it fails, since nobody
would agree to be imprisoned for the colour of their eyes. A checkable trigger could
never have told the two rules apart.

On facts, Rawls and I are closer than a quick reading suggests. His limits on public
reason cover constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, not whether a
particular accused did it, and in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) he treats a criminal trial
as a procedure that aims at the true verdict and is imperfect because it can miss.
That a rule's trigger must answer to reality is something I share with him, not a
correction of him.

Where I part from him is the bar, and the door. Rawls distinguishes an overlapping
consensus from a mere *modus vivendi*, a truce that holds only while the balance of
interests holds, and ranks the truce below it. My threshold, grudging acceptance by
people who could leave but judge staying worth more, sits close to what he would call a
modus vivendi. I accept it as enough for a rule to count as defended among real people,
and I keep the stricter role-reversal test as the better standard to write rules by.
Rawls sets the bar at reasonable endorsement. And Rawls treats society as closed,
entered by birth and left by death, and denies that the bare right to emigrate makes
anyone's acceptance voluntary. I keep exit in the picture, because my threshold is
defined by it. Toward those who do not want in, the shared norms are silent: force can
still resolve the conflict, but it borrows no legitimacy from the frame. My bar is lower
and less comfortable than his; I think it is closer to how real people actually manage to
live together, and it says plainly where justification runs out.

There is a harder case outside the shared framework. Real agents face time and
resource pressure, and negotiating a shared frame is often infeasible and sometimes
impossible. There, force is the ultimate conflict resolver. It resolves; it does not
justify, and winning confers no legitimacy. The other party's suffering retains its
pro tanto weight, and my institutional prohibition on torture remains absolute
([A0.P1](/assumptions#a0-p1), [A0.P2](/assumptions#a0-p2)). Those commitments do not
depend on the other party agreeing to my framework.

Better information can remove factual disagreements and reveal opportunities for
peaceful agreement. It does not guarantee compatible aims or a mutually acceptable
settlement, even if the information is perfect. Where aims remain incompatible,
this page does not make them compatible. Nor does it supply an available shared
tribunal where none exists. The practical possibility of imposing an outcome and
the normative task of defending it remain distinct.

## 5. The tool, used once

A pattern you have seen this year, whatever year you are reading in. A public number comes
out — a rate, a count, a cost. One side embraces it without checking. The other side's reply
begins and ends with who produced it. Run my test on both moves.

The embrace fails quietly, and usually innocently: an unchecked number is most often
accepted out of ordinary trust in a familiar source, trust nobody has calibrated. But a
number nobody checked answers to the source, not the world, and if it turns out right it
merely got lucky about the direction. The rejection, when it fails, fails loudly, and notice
exactly where: not at "who produced it?", which is a fair question with a checkable answer,
but at what happens next. Asked "so is the instrument broken, and how?", it may have
nothing, and that alone proves little; not everyone can say how an instrument fails. The
second question is the one that tells: "what would a measurement you *would* accept look
like?" If the answer is still nothing, and no measurement from them could ever count, then
its content was never "the world is otherwise," or even "this thermometer is broken"; it was
"no number from them can bind us." In neither move, as described, does the evidence do the
deciding: one rests on trust nobody has checked, the other on a refusal nothing could
answer. The alternative I am asking for
is a shared practice of checking claims and correcting mistakes. We can work toward
that even while disagreeing about some of the philosophy offered on this page.

## 6. What I hope we can share

My own commitment remains that there is a way things are, independent of what
anyone prefers. I have tried to show what that commitment contributes to inquiry
and to the scrutiny of coercive rules.

What I hope to share with you is a practical commitment: seek workable common
standards, let evidence correct either side, and keep public rules and their
application open to challenge. Agreement does not make every rule just; the rules
still need a defence, and uncertainty does not disappear because we cooperate.

You do not have to accept this whole project to join that effort. If building a
shared framework seems desirable to you, even while much of my philosophy does
not, that is a worthwhile outcome of this essay. The arguments that follow remain
open to your criticism.
