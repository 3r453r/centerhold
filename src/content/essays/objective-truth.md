*Builds on [the Assumptions](/assumptions).*[^base]

I want to begin with the sentence everything I write here hangs on: **there is a way things
are.**[^a01] Not a way for you and a way for me — a way, full stop, indifferent to preference,
party, or mood. The world was some way before anyone was here to have opinions about it, and
it will be some way after.

That is where my philosophy starts. What I am after reaches further, and it is practical: a
shared framework, meaning standards of evidence and public rules that people on different
sides accept, in which claims can be checked, mistakes corrected, and coercive rules
challenged. You may disagree with my account of why those practices work. If you come away
thinking that framework worth building, while rejecting the rest of this project, this essay
will still have achieved something I want.

## 1. What I am claiming, and what I am not

The claim is narrow. *There is a way things are* says nothing about who knows it, how well
anyone can know it, or how confident anyone is entitled to be. Those are separate questions,
and I give them the less comfortable answers: plenty of truths are out of reach, our
instruments are partial, our minds are motivated, and honest inquiry moves toward the facts
slowly, closing in on them with no promise of arriving, when it moves at all. None of that
touches the claim. A hidden fact is still a fact. The number of fish in the Atlantic at this
moment is unknown and unknowable in practice; it is also exactly one number.

Notice what the claim rules out, because a claim that makes no difference anywhere says
nothing, and I hold my own claim to that rule first.[^a02] Mine makes no observation more or
less likely; its difference is in what we may conclude. It rules out *your truth and my
truth* as descriptions of the world. It permits, cheerfully, your perspective and mine, your
values and mine, your experience and mine: perspectives differ because we stand in different
places, not because there are different worlds to stand in. Two witnesses to a car crash
disagree, and we can say something precise about that: of any two of their claims that
contradict each other, at most one is right. The crash happened one way.

A second commitment keeps apart two things I lean on throughout. One is the map: our
description of what is there, which the world can correct. The other is the path we choose
across it, which I call policy: what we decide, rather than what we find.[^d2] Keeping them
apart rules out a comfortable retreat: "well, that is true for science, but this domain is
different." A domain where nothing is the case is not a deeper domain; it is empty of facts,
and should say so. Most real domains are neither wholly one nor the other: part found, part
chosen. My own ethics is mixed in exactly this way. That suffering is bad for the one who
suffers, I take to be found; much of the rest of morality, I take to be chosen. Wherever
there is a fact of the matter, disagreement is a problem with a possible resolution.
Wherever there is not, we are no longer describing — we are choosing, and I insist on saying
so out loud. I keep the map and the path apart everywhere.

## 2. The referee

Now watch what the sentence *does*. Two people disagree about something that matters — a
dosage, a bridge load, whether the well water is safe. If there is a way things are, their
disagreement has a referee. Not a person: a direction. There is something the dispute is
*about*, sitting outside both of them, that their claims can be checked against. They can be
wrong. They can find out. The argument can *end* — not by one side tiring, but by the world
answering.

Every practice we trust works this way. Engineering is argument refereed by collapse.
Medicine is argument refereed by mortality. Navigation is argument refereed by the coastline.
The referee is often slow, often expensive to consult, sometimes cruel — but it is there, and
both parties accept the same ways of consulting it. Those shared tests and standards of
evidence, not a shared philosophy of what is real, are what make their disagreement a
*conversation* rather than a contest. The loser of a refereed argument has not been defeated
by the winner. He has been corrected by the world, and the winner is answerable to the same
referee on the next question. That is why refereed losing is survivable, even useful.
Nobody submits to a person; both submit to the way things are.

It does not always end that way, and I will not pretend otherwise. Some disagreements run all
the way down to where argument no longer tells the two sides apart: each stands on a last
premise, and neither has anything left that the other is obliged to accept. ("Last" marks
where these two stopped, not where the reasons end.) There the world does not answer, or does
not answer yet.

Even there, my opening sentence, a mild one as metaphysics goes, still buys something. Where
those last premises are claims about the world, the world is some way on the point the two of
them stopped short of: of two premises that contradict each other, at most one is true, and
the deadlock is ignorance, not two equally good answers. Where a last premise is an aim rather than a claim
about the world, we have crossed from the map to the path, and there may be no fact to find.
That much follows from the mild sentence alone, and it binds everyone who grants the sentence,
me included. Against someone who refuses it, the sentence is itself my last premise, set against
theirs. That limits what this argument proves; it does not show that we cannot cooperate on a
practical question.

Will the ignorance lift? I expect sustained inquiry tested against reality to improve our
grasp of particular questions over time. What supports the expectation is a requirement: a
theory that replaces another has to account for the older theory's well-supported successes,
where they were tested. The expectation is a further, weaker commitment, and it does not promise that any
particular ignorance will lift.[^a07]

Someone who does not accept all of my account of what is real may still accept tests that can
correct either of us: sharing workable standards of evidence and public rules does not require
us to agree on every question about what is ultimately real.[^035] Two investigators could
disagree about whether a reality no one can ever reach is in some definite state, and still
agree on how to test the water. That gives them something useful to do together without
settling the deeper question first. So before treating a philosophical disagreement as a
refusal to answer to evidence, we should find out what the other person actually accepts.

## 3. The tell

Here is a pattern you have seen this year, whatever year you are reading in. A public number
comes out — a rate, a count, a cost. One side embraces it without checking. The other side
dislikes what it shows.

There are more honest responses to a number you dislike than the usual fight admits. *The
measurement is wrong, and here is a better one.* *The measurement is right, and here is why it
means something else.* And a third, which I want to protect: *the measurement is unreliable,
here is why, and I do not yet know the true value.* A broken thermometer can be thrown out
before you own a working one. Who funded a study, how its sample was chosen, who declined to
answer, whether the thing counted is the thing claimed — all of that is evidence about
reliability, and asking after it is doing the map's work, not leaving the map. All three
responses accept the referee; all three can lose.

The tell is none of these. It is when the story of where a number came from is offered as a
*complete substitute* for looking at the number: "the number is theirs" is the whole reply,
and no correction the other side could make, no re-measurement by anyone, would let the result
count. Such a reply, if that is all there is, says nothing about the world. It does not say
the world is other than measured, or that the instrument is broken in some way a better
instrument could fix. Allegiance to the claimant, or hostility toward them, has taken the place
of an assessment of the claim.[^tell]

Now go back to the public number, suppose the other side's reply is just that, and run the
test on both moves. The embrace fails quietly, and usually innocently: an unchecked number is
most often accepted out of ordinary trust in a familiar source, trust nobody has calibrated.
But a number nobody checked answers to the source, not the world; if it turns out right, that
was luck.

The rejection, when it fails, fails loudly, and at a precise point: not at "who produced
it?", which is a fair question with a checkable answer, but at what comes next. The first follow-up is
"so is the instrument broken, and how?", and having no answer to it proves little on its own:
the speaker may lack the expertise to say, or may be fairly discounting a source whose track
record has earned a discount. The question that tells is the second: "what would a
measurement you *would* accept look like?" If the answer is nothing, and no measurement from
them could ever count, then the reply was never "the world is otherwise," or even "this
thermometer is broken"; it was "no number from them can bind us."

In neither move, as described, does the evidence do the deciding: one rests on trust nobody
has checked, the other on a refusal nothing could answer. Investigating bias is legitimate.
Refusing to count a result solely because it comes from the other side, whatever checks or
corrections are offered, blocks the shared practice I am asking for: checking claims and
correcting mistakes.[^bias]

## 4. What force must answer to

Coercion needs a justification, and the facts are one part of it. Whether the accusation against someone is
true is one question; whether the rule under which they would be coerced is justified is
another.

Take two public rules: a speed limit, and a rule that people with blue eyes must be
imprisoned. Each could have a perfectly checkable trigger, the fact that sets it off: a
driver's speed can be measured, and so can the colour of someone's eyes. Applying the
blue-eyes rule with perfect accuracy would not justify it. What a rule permits has to be
defended too, and that defence belongs to policy; it cannot be read straight off a
measurement.

Here is the policy I defend. Inside a shared framework, a rule that coerces people must be
stated publicly, and the facts that trigger it must be open to scrutiny.[^p4]

Inside the framework, factual scrutiny constrains how a rule is applied. An accusation must answer to evidence about
something neither side controls. The accuser can be wrong, and the process must leave room to
challenge the accusation. That takes justified standards of evidence and procedures for
challenge, not a perfect instrument, and those standards need a defence of their own: an
uncertain danger may justify a precaution, while what evidence would justify punishment is a
further question.[^scrutiny]

That is the link between truth and force, and its limit. A claim about what happened must not
become true merely because the more powerful party asserts it. But neither a correct
accusation nor a public rule is enough, on its own, to establish that a particular use of
force is justified.

What does defend a rule? My answer is conditional. The norms of a shared life bind whoever
wants in, and are silent toward those who don't. Inside it, a coercive rule is
defended when the people who want in could accept it as a term of living together, whatever
else they believe about the world.[^defend] This is not a new idea: John Rawls argued that
people holding incompatible worldviews can still share the terms of a political life, and the
cooperation I hope for is close to his in aim.

"Could accept" includes grudging acceptance. A rule may cut against someone's convictions,
but not so far that they would rather leave the shared life, because staying is worth more to
them by their own weighing. That is the threshold for a rule to count as defended. It is lower
and less comfortable than Rawls's, which asks for reasonable endorsement; I think it is closer
to how real people actually manage to live together, and it says plainly where justification
runs out.[^rawls] The test I prefer is stricter, and I hold it the better standard to write
rules by: if this rule were in place, would I agree to be its subject? It is the old
role-reversal form of the Golden Rule. Both are practical standards for real people living
together, not a claim to have found the morally right rule for perfectly informed, idealised
agents.

The threshold only works for people who can choose. If you can neither reject a rule nor
leave, the rule is not a policy you are party to; for you it is part of the terrain, like the
weather. It is still a policy for whoever imposes it, and they owe an answer. Imposed on
people who cannot realistically leave, a rule has to pass the stricter test: would I, the one
imposing it, agree to be its subject? And it stays bounded by the limits that never depend on
anyone's agreement: established suffering always counts, and the institutional ban on torture
stays absolute.[^noexit] Taking someone's options away when you could leave them open is
itself a wrong, even if the person does not know what they are missing and is content: seen
with the full picture in view, they are deprived of ways they could have grown.

Now run the two rules from the start of this section through both tests. The speed limit:
the driver who likes speed accepts it grudgingly rather than emigrate, so it passes the
threshold; and if I picture a child near the road, I would agree to be fined under it, so it
passes the stricter test too. The blue-eyes rule: a blue-eyed person who could leave would
rather go than live under it, so it fails the threshold; for one who cannot leave, the
threshold is silent, and the rule meets the stricter test instead, which it fails, since
nobody would agree to be imprisoned for the colour of their eyes. A checkable trigger could
never have told the two rules apart.

Outside a shared framework, the case is harder. In the real world each side faces pressure of
time and resources, and negotiating a shared framework is often infeasible and sometimes
impossible. There, force is the ultimate conflict resolver. It resolves; it does not justify,
and winning confers no legitimacy. The same two limits still hold: the other side's suffering
still counts, and the institutional ban on torture I hold remains absolute. Neither depends on
the other side agreeing to my framework.[^outside]

Better information can remove factual disagreements and show where a peaceful agreement is
possible. But even perfect information does not guarantee that two sides want compatible
things, or a settlement both can accept.[^info] Where their aims remain incompatible, this page
does not make them compatible. Nor does it supply a shared tribunal where none exists.

## 5. What I hope we can share

My own commitment remains that there is a way things are, independent of what anyone
prefers. What I hope to share with you is a practical commitment: seek workable common
standards, let evidence correct either side, and keep public rules and their application open
to challenge. You do not have to accept this whole project to join that effort. Agreement does
not make every rule just; the rules still need a defence, and uncertainty does not disappear
because we cooperate. The arguments that follow remain open to your criticism.

about a group can tell us about the person in front of you, and why evidence, treatment,
and publication require distinct judgments.

    *Political Liberalism* (1993) he argued that people holding incompatible worldviews can
    still share the terms of a political life, and that political power is fully legitimate
    only when it is exercised under a constitution whose essentials all citizens could
    reasonably be expected to endorse. He hoped for an overlapping consensus: each worldview
    endorsing those shared terms from within, for its own moral reasons. On facts we are
    closer than a quick reading suggests. His limits on public reason cover constitutional
    essentials and matters of basic justice, not whether a particular accused did it, and in
    *A Theory of Justice* (1971) he treats a criminal trial as a procedure that aims at the
    true verdict and is imperfect because it can miss. That a rule's trigger must answer to
    reality is something I share with him, not a correction of him. Where I part from him is
    the bar, and the door. He distinguishes an overlapping consensus from a mere *modus
    vivendi*, a truce that holds only while the balance of interests holds, and ranks the
    truce below it. My threshold sits close to what he would call a modus vivendi; I accept it
    as enough for a rule to count as defended among real people, and I keep the stricter
    role-reversal test as the better standard to write rules by. Rawls sets the bar at
    reasonable endorsement. And he treats society as closed, entered by birth and left by
    death, and denies that the bare right to emigrate makes anyone's acceptance voluntary. I
    keep exit in the picture, because my threshold is defined by it. Toward those who do not
    want in, the shared norms are silent: force can still resolve the conflict, but it borrows
    no legitimacy from the frame.
