I'm not going to tell you why markets fell on 5 November 2026, because I didn't look into it. This page is about something smaller: what the numbers on a fund's page were measuring that day. A Korea fund reported a fall more than twice the Korean market's, and the next day reported a rise while Korea fell another 6%. Two funds on one index differed by five points for the same date. Different clocks and valuation methods can produce all of it, and the figures alone don't show that anyone made a mistake.

Two terms first. A fund's net asset value (NAV) is what its holdings are worth per share, worked out once a day. All the funds here are exchange-traded, so you don't trade them at it: you trade at the market price, which can sit above or below. And by "points" I mean percentage points.

If you hold one of these and don't trade, which estimate the page prints doesn't change the shares inside it, though their prices still move. It matters when you compare funds' falls, and when you buy or sell, because you pay the live price. (Ordinary funds that deal once a day work differently, and I didn't look at them.) There's a short checklist further down.

## Three numbers

iShares' page for EWY, its MSCI South Korea fund, gave these for 5 November:

| | 4 Nov | 5 Nov | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net asset value (NAV), as published | 154.76 | 133.58 | -13.68% |
| Market close, New York | 152.12 | 131.65 | -13.46% |
| "Non-FV NAV", also on the page | not shown | 146.59 | |

Korea's main index, the KOSPI, fell 5.28% that day. So a Korean stock fund reported a fall more than twice the size of the Korean market's on the same date.

Seoul had shut before New York opened. New York then fell hard: the S&P 500 lost 4.36%, nearly all of it after the open, and SOXX, a chip-stock fund, 12.15%, more than a third of that before the open. A fund priced in New York has to say at 4 p.m. New York what the shares it holds are worth, when their own market closed hours ago. iShares says how it handles that, on the page: the published NAV "uses systematic fair value to price certain foreign assets after the close of the local markets to the price that might have prevailed as of 4:00 p.m., Eastern time". The non-fair-value figure "will instead use local closing market prices for those foreign assets and foreign exchange rates as of 4:00 p.m., London time". "Fair value" is the industry's term for this estimate. It doesn't mean the correct value.

So 146.59 is roughly what the holdings closed at in Seoul, in dollars at a London-afternoon exchange rate. Only roughly: iShares says the non-fair-value figure can still carry some adjustments in limited cases, such as holdings that are suspended or have no price, and I can't tell whether any applied that day. Adding up the day's holdings file gives 146.43. 133.58 is an estimate of what they'd have been worth had Seoul still been open, at New York's closing exchange rate, so some of the distance between them is currency, and I have no clean figure for how much. 131.65 is what the last trades paid, and it was the day's low: EWY opened at 144.94. iShares adds that "Investors cannot transact with the Fund at NFV NAV", so 146.59 is a valuation for information, not a price anyone could deal with the fund at. It sits about 11% above 131.65.

## Same index, same date, same issuer

Here are two iShares funds tracking one index, MSCI Emerging Markets, for the same date:

| Fund | Domicile | Index that day, on the page | NAV change |
|---|---|---|---|
| EEM | United States | no daily figure | -5.77% |
| IEEM | Ireland | -0.86% | -0.89% |

That's nearly five points between them. IEEM's NAV followed its index to within a few hundredths. EEM's is the one that moved away. Over the previous month, EEM's NAV had averaged about 1.004 times IEEM's. On 5 November, EEM's non-fair-value NAV was also 1.004 times IEEM's. That fits the two funds agreeing on local closes, though it sets one day against an average from 1 October to 3 November of a ratio that ranged from 0.963 to 1.016 over its last eight days. Most of the five points is therefore fair value and a later exchange rate. A little under one of them was there already: on 4 November EEM's NAV stood about 0.9% above that usual ratio, though the ratio swings a lot.

IEEM's prospectus gives a reason it would follow its index: it's valued "using the index methodology of valuing securities", at "the last traded price for equity securities". Where those prices don't, "in the opinion of the Manager, reflect their fair value", the directors or an appointed valuer set the value, so the power exists, but it's written as an exception. EEM's page describes fair value as how it works, and the Irish funds' pages I read don't mention it. I read that one prospectus, and I can't tell from it whether the power was used that day.

London adds a third clock. EIMI, an iShares fund listed there, on a wider emerging-markets index, had a NAV down 0.93% and a market close down 3.76%, finishing about 2.1% under its NAV, having finished 0.8% over it the day before. London shuts about four and a half hours before New York, so people there may have been trading on a fall the index hadn't caught yet, though I can't separate that from everything else that moved that day. It's a discount to a differently timed number, which doesn't show EIMI was cheap.

## The next day

Thursday was 5 November. Here's the same table with Friday's figures:

| | 5 Nov | 6 Nov | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net asset value (NAV), as published | 133.58 | 137.13 | +2.66% |
| Market close, New York | 131.65 | 137.01 | +4.07% |
| "Non-FV NAV" | 146.59 | 136.13 | -7.14% |

The 11% spread is now under 1%. The KOSPI fell another 6.02% on Friday, and the non-fair-value NAV, on Seoul's closes and a London-afternoon exchange rate, fell 7.14%. The published NAV rose. So a fund that had reported more than twice Korea's fall on Thursday reported a rise on the day Korea fell 6%. EWT, iShares' Taiwan fund, did the same: published NAV +3.55%, non-fair-value NAV -3.97%, with Taiwan's main index down 4.51%. EEM was milder, +2.03% and -2.69%.

Over the two days from 4 November:

| 4 to 6 Nov | Change |
|---|---|
| KOSPI, in won | -11.0% |
| KOSPI in dollars, at Yahoo's rough rate | about -12% |
| EWY's holdings file on 4 Nov, to the non-fair-value NAV on 6 Nov | -11.8% |
| EWY's published NAV | -11.4% |
| EWY's market close | -9.9% |

It's tempting to call that one fall, reported a day apart, and I wouldn't lean on it. The last three rows end within 1% of each other on Friday, so they were always going to look alike, and the close differs mainly because it started 1.71% under its NAV on 4 November and ended about 0.1% under on the 6th. Friday's NAV is struck hours after Seoul's close, and I can't isolate what moved in between. And the two NAVs didn't only meet by Seoul falling: the published NAV's rise did about a quarter of the closing in EWY, and over 40% in EWT and EEM. EWY is the tidiest of the three.

The Irish side is the check on that. IEEM's NAV fell 2.65% on 6 November, so over the two days it's down 3.52%, against 3.86% for EEM's. Over the two days, then, they differ by about a third of a point, against almost five on 5 November alone. Day by day they didn't converge: Friday's changes still differed by about 4.7 points, the other way, and EEM's NAV ended about 0.5% above its usual ratio to IEEM's. EIMI, the London fund from earlier, on its wider index, had a NAV down 2.58% on 6 November, which makes 3.49% over the two days. Its London price is down about 3.2% over the same two days, so price and NAV ended close to where they started against each other: 0.8% over on 4 November, about 1.1% over on 6 November. In between, at 2.1% under, came the gap of the 5th, and it reversed on the 6th, when the price rose about 0.6% while the NAV fell. The London prices are Yahoo's daily closes (48.91 on 6 November).





## How often

I expected 5 November to be the extreme. By some measures it was: EWY's -13.68% and EWT's -11.26% are the largest NAV moves in their series since January 2025, and EEM against IEEM is the second-widest of 455 days. By the measure this page is about, it wasn't the largest, though it was near the top.

EIMI's gap between price and NAV was typically 0.37% over the 1,261 days back to November 2021, and 0.62% so far in 2026. 5 November ranks 15th, inside the widest 1.2%, and 9th in the last 260 days. Gaps of 1% or more have become far commoner: 63 of 215 days in 2026, against 15 of 253 in 2025. The widest was 4.49%, on 4 April 2025.

For EWY, I set the fund's NAV change against the KOSPI in dollars each day. That's a rough stand-in for Seoul's close, and Yahoo's daily exchange rate looks like a midnight-London snapshot, so I ran it three ways: with that day's rate, with the next day's, and in won with no rate at all. Call the difference the gap. Here's how often it was 2 points or more:

| Period | Days, lowest and highest of the three |
|---|---|
| January to June 2025 | 7 to 17 of about 109 |
| July to December 2025 | 6 to 13 of 121 |
| January to June 2026 | 29 to 45 of 117 |
| July to 5 November 2026 | 36 to 43 of 84 |

(The 13 includes one ex-dividend day.) So roughly one day in ten last year, nearly one in two since July, and many of those count twice, because one move shows up in the NAV one day and in Seoul the next. The daily swing in the KOSPI in dollars more than doubled, which fits Korea's rise: measured against each half's own swing instead of a fixed 2 points, Korea's gap days hold at 17% to 18% in every half on the same-day rate; on the next-day rate they run 9 to 14% and with no exchange rate 15 to 21%, so that steadiness is on one version only. It doesn't fit EWT, whose 2-point days also went up, to between 26% and 35% in early 2026 and 20% to 23% since July, with Taiwan's swing no higher than in early 2025, though above late 2025's. One thing does track it: the US session, which starts after Taipei has shut. With that day's rate, EWT's daily gap correlates .55 with the Philadelphia semiconductor index's move from open to close on the same date, and -.58 with the previous date's; the gap moves about half a point for each point of that day's move. By itself, that day's move accounts for about a third of the gap's day-to-day variation. That index moved 3% or more either way, open to close, in 18 of 123 sessions in the first half of 2026, against 12 of 122 in the first half of 2025, 6 of 128 in the second and 7 of 91 since July. I picked that threshold after seeing the results, and by the standard deviation of those moves the wildest half was early 2025, not early 2026. So it tracks part of the rise, not all of it: since July, EWT has had more 2-point days than early 2025 with fewer big US sessions. It's a correlation over one fund's two years, not a cause I can show, and I've read only the filings' description of how iShares sets its fair values, not the model's inputs. 5 November ranks between 4th and 7th, depending on the rate; the largest was 31 July 2026 on all three, when Yahoo has the KOSPI up about 18% in a day and the fund's NAV up 4%.

Does the next day bear the estimate out? In direction, mostly: after a 2-point gap, Seoul's next move had the gap's sign about three times in four (74% to 80%), and Taiwan's 67% to 79%. In size, less: the median miss is 2.3 to 2.5 points in Korea and 1.6 to 2.0 in Taiwan, and the gap mostly overshoots, since the next move was larger than the gap on only 35% to 38% of those days in Korea and 12% to 18% in Taiwan. I'd call it a useful guess, not a price anyone was going to get. And it's a look back over matched days, with any date missing from a series dropped, not a forecast anyone could have run at the time: I don't know when Yahoo's exchange rates were fixed.

## What I'd take from it

These gaps don't need a violent day: since January 2025, EEM's and IEEM's changes differed by a point or more on about one day in four. On a violent day they change the story. Before comparing two funds' falls, or a fund's fall with its index, I'd check four things:

- **Whose clock is the date on?** The same label on a US and an Irish fund can mean prices of different ages.
- **Is that NAV a local close or an estimate?** Some pages tell you, and they may print both.
- **Is the number a NAV or a price?** You trade at the price. The price can sit away from NAV, either way, and that gap isn't automatically a bargain.
- **Which index, in which currency?** The KOSPI is in won and isn't EWY's index. It happened not to matter much on 5 November. It can.

I looked closely at one day and a handful of funds, and at the longer series only for the funds on this page, over a stretch that includes some unusually violent months. iShares itself warns that fair value can make a fund's return diverge from its benchmark's.

## For anyone who wants the test

I said the next day bears the estimate out "in direction, mostly". That comes partly cheap. Many gaps are echoes, opposite-sign leftovers of the day before, when Seoul is just catching up (35% to 43% of them, depending on the rate), and on most echo days betting with the gap and betting on reversal are the same bet. Some of the rest is plain reversal: a local market's next move simply going against its last one. Here are those apart, with three more iShares funds added:

| Next local move has the gap's sign | Korea | Taiwan | Japan, Hong Kong, Australia, pooled |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaps that aren't echoes | 83% to 88% | 78% to 85% | 77% to 83% |
| Betting on reversal, same days | 48% to 58% | 54% to 61% | 69% to 73% |
| Echoes | 58% to 71% | 38% to 65% | 62% to 76% |

(Echoes are 16 to 50 days per column; on the other days the two bets disagree on 10 to 25 of them, and there the gap's sign won 79% or more.) I split Korea and Taiwan after seeing the averages. The last column is the check: predictions written down first, on funds I hadn't looked at. The gap's sign carries, though by less, since reversal does much better there, and the pooling hides a lot: Japan alone is 69% to 77%, Australia 88% to 97% on as few as 9 days. Echoes being weaker doesn't clearly carry: I'd predicted at least 10 points on the rate I'd named in advance, and it came out 7.5, with 18.6 on another version and 0.2 on the third. Those aren't one sample measured three ways: changing the rate changes which days count, and on the rate I'd named, Japan's echoes did better than its other gaps, while Hong Kong had only two. Two days on, the test across all gaps gives 48% to 57% in Korea, so it can fail there, and 58% to 61% in Taiwan, which leaves Taiwan's result weaker. None of this rules out a bad figure for one day's index, which would push that day's gap and the next day's move the same way.

Thursday's gap had the right sign and overshot. In won, with no exchange rate, Korea's next move was 2.4 points smaller than the gap, about its median miss of 2.3 to 2.5, and Taiwan's 3.6 points smaller, worse than its 1.6 to 2.0. The dollar versions move with Yahoo's exchange rates for those days, which were still being revised when I read them. Friday's NAV change differed from Seoul's by about 9 to 10 points, the other way. That's an echo, so I read nothing into it for the next session.
